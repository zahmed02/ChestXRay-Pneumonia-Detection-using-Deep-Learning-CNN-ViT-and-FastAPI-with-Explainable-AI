# Pneumonia Radiographic Findings

Consolidation appears on a chest X-ray as an area of increased opacity (whiteness) that obscures the underlying lung markings, typically caused by fluid, pus, or cells filling the alveoli instead of air. It usually follows the anatomical boundaries of a lung lobe or segment.

Air bronchograms are a classic sign of consolidation: air-filled bronchi appear as dark, branching lines against the surrounding white, fluid-filled alveolar tissue. Their presence strongly supports an alveolar (airspace) process such as pneumonia rather than a purely interstitial one.

The silhouette sign describes the loss of a normally sharp border (such as the heart border or diaphragm outline) when an adjacent structure of similar density — like a consolidated lung segment — sits directly against it. Its location can help localize which lobe is affected: loss of the right heart border suggests right middle lobe disease, while loss of the diaphragm outline suggests lower lobe disease.

Bronchopneumonia (lobular pneumonia) presents as patchy, multifocal opacities scattered through one or more lobes, often bilateral, and is more common in children and the elderly. Lobar pneumonia presents as a single, more homogeneous opacity confined to one lobe.

Interstitial patterns (reticular or reticulonodular markings without frank consolidation or air bronchograms) are more typical of viral or atypical pneumonias than bacterial lobar pneumonia, though there is significant overlap and radiographic appearance alone cannot reliably distinguish the causative organism.
