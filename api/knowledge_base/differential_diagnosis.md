# Differential Diagnosis for Chest X-Ray Opacities

Not all lung opacities represent pneumonia. Atelectasis (partial lung collapse) can produce a similar area of increased density, but is often accompanied by volume loss signs such as shifted fissures, elevated diaphragm, or mediastinal shift toward the opacity, whereas pneumonia typically does not cause volume loss.

Pulmonary edema, from causes such as heart failure, typically produces bilateral, symmetric, perihilar ("bat-wing") opacities, often with associated cardiomegaly, Kerley B lines, and vascular redistribution — a different pattern from the typically asymmetric, lobar or patchy distribution of infectious pneumonia.

Pleural effusion appears as blunting of the costophrenic angle or a meniscus sign, and can occur either on its own (e.g., from heart failure or malignancy) or alongside pneumonia as a parapneumonic effusion; its presence does not by itself confirm an infectious process.

Pulmonary contusion, aspiration, and post-obstructive changes can also mimic consolidation radiographically. Clinical context (fever, symptom onset, recent trauma, aspiration risk) is essential alongside the imaging appearance, since chest X-ray findings alone are frequently non-specific.
